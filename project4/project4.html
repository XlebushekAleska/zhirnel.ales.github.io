Part 01

Game Theory Overview

In a strategic game, each player's result depends on the strategies chosen by all participants.

Normal-form game

For two players, a normal-form game can be represented as a matrix. Player 1 chooses a row, Player 2 chooses a column, and every cell contains a pair of payoffs.

(u₁, u₂) = (Player 1 payoff, Player 2 payoff)
Interpretation

Higher payoff = more preferred outcome

A payoff does not have to represent money. It can represent utility, points, profit, years with a minus sign, or any other numerical preference scale.

Nash equilibrium

A strategy profile is a Nash equilibrium when neither player can improve their own payoff by changing only their strategy while the other player's strategy stays fixed.

Each player's strategy is a best response to the other.
Course example

Prisoner's Dilemma

The preset from the course material uses Confess and Silent as the two strategies. Its Nash equilibrium is worse for both players than mutual silence, illustrating the difference between individual stability and a collectively preferable outcome.

Part 02

Best Responses and Pareto Optimality

The analyzer marks the local incentives that create Nash equilibria and separately checks whether outcomes are Pareto-optimal.

Best response

For every fixed strategy of Player 2, Player 1 compares all rows and chooses the highest Player 1 payoff. Player 2 does the same across columns for every fixed row.

Nash test

Two best responses meet

If one cell is a best response for both players at the same time, that cell is a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium. Ties are allowed, so one game can have several pure Nash equilibria.

Pareto optimality

An outcome is Pareto-dominated if another outcome makes neither player worse off and makes at least one player strictly better off. An outcome is Pareto-optimal when no such improvement exists.

Important limitation

Pure strategies only

This calculator searches for Nash equilibria in pure strategies. Some finite games have no pure equilibrium and require mixed strategies, where players randomize between several actions.

Part 04

Behaviour Simulation

A small Monte Carlo experiment inspired by the Person and Crime classes in the course manual.

Random decisions

The course manual models a regular person's confession as an approximately 50% random event and a crime-ring member's decision to rat out as a much rarer event. Here both probabilities can be changed explicitly.

Monte Carlo idea

Repeat the same random experiment many times

With more trials, the observed percentage usually moves closer to the probability specified by the model.
